# Kentucky Appellate Decisions – Published and Unpublished Summaries

## Published Appellate Decisions

### CertainTeed Corp. v. Dexter, 330 S.W\.3d 64 (Ky. 2010)

In this Kentucky Supreme Court case, a \$5 million asbestos verdict was initially thrown out by the trial judge due to the jury’s failure to apportion any fault to the numerous “empty-chair” defendants (settled or dismissed parties). The Supreme Court reinstated the trial judge’s order for a new trial, holding that there was *substantial evidence* that those other companies had also contributed to the plaintiff’s lung cancer, so the first jury’s allocation of 0% fault to them was “manifestly unsupported by the evidence”. The Court clarified that a defendant seeking to apportion fault to absent tortfeasors must present sufficient proof of each absent party’s liability (duty and causation) – essentially the same quantum of proof required as if they were present defendants. Because evidence showed **“each and every exposure”** to asbestos was a contributing cause of the illness, any proven exposure attributable to the empty-chair defendants meant the jury *should* have assigned them a portion of fault. The Supreme Court emphasized that trial courts have broad discretion in granting a new trial, and an appellate court should defer to such decisions when the record supports them. Ultimately, the Court reversed the Court of Appeals and ordered that the new trial (which had resulted in a lower total award but apportioned 21% fault to the absent defendants) be upheld.

### Clephas v. Garlock, Inc., 168 S.W\.3d 389 (Ky. App. 2004)

This case arose from a jury verdict in favor of Garlock in an asbestos exposure lawsuit brought by former pipefitter Charles Clephas and his wife. The Kentucky Court of Appeals vacated that defense verdict and ordered a new trial due to **discovery violations and evidentiary errors**. Garlock had failed to adequately disclose the specific opinions of its medical expert, Dr. Robert Sawyer, before trial. Instead, Garlock provided only a broad, generic outline of Dr. Sawyer’s anticipated testimony, and then did not produce him for a pre-trial deposition. At trial, Dr. Sawyer went on to offer new opinions – including that Mr. Clephas did *not* have asbestosis and that any lung issues were due to smoking or asthma – which the plaintiffs had no fair notice of. The Court of Appeals held it was an abuse of discretion to allow this “trial by surprise.” Kentucky’s civil rules require parties to disclose not just the expert’s name but *“the substance of the facts and opinions”* to which the expert will testify. Garlock’s generic disclosure violated both the letter and spirit of those rules, and the nondisclosure clearly prejudiced the plaintiffs. The court noted that *“the discovery of the substance of an expert witness’s expected testimony is essential to trial preparation,”* and that Garlock’s “persistent pattern of noncompliance” forced the plaintiffs to fend off ambush opinions at trial. Accordingly, the verdict was vacated. The Court of Appeals also reviewed the trial court’s admission of testimony by Garlock’s industrial hygienist expert (Donna Ringo), who had opined that removing asbestos gaskets released minimal fibers. The court found no abuse of discretion in admitting her testimony, even though her studies had not been published or peer-reviewed – the trial judge acted within his gatekeeping authority in deeming her methodology (though “a close call”) sufficiently reliable. In sum, the new trial was ordered due to the undisclosed expert opinions from Dr. Sawyer, which the appellate court deemed **sufficiently prejudicial** to require reversal.

### Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp. v. Golightly, 976 S.W\.2d 409 (Ky. 1998)

In this products liability suit, a former insulator (Golightly) won a judgment against Owens-Corning for illnesses caused by asbestos exposure, including \$290,000 in compensatory damages and \$435,000 in punitive damages. Owens-Corning appealed the punitive award, arguing it was excessive and unconstitutional—especially given the company had faced multiple asbestos lawsuits. The Kentucky Supreme Court disagreed and **upheld the punitive damages**, using this case to clarify due-process limits in mass torts. The Court noted compelling evidence that Owens-Corning knew for decades that its asbestos insulation (“Kaylo” pipe insulation) was deadly, yet continued to sell it without warnings and even falsely marketed it as *“non-irritating, non-toxic”*. Rejecting the company’s “state-of-the-art” defense, the Court held that an entire industry cannot escape punitive liability merely because *“every member of that industry”* was doing the same dangerous thing. As to the constitutional issue, the Court joined the majority of jurisdictions in holding that **multiple punitive damage awards for the same misconduct are not per se forbidden** in mass tort cases. Each asbestos plaintiff’s case involves unique harm, so punishing a defendant in one case doesn’t immunize it from punitive liability to other victims. The decision cited BMW v. Gore but found the 1.5:1 ratio of punitive-to-compensatory damages here (435k on 290k) was nowhere near “grossly excessive”. Kentucky law requires considering both the defendant’s conduct and the harm to *this* plaintiff, and here the jury could reasonably find Owens-Corning’s conduct particularly egregious toward Mr. Golightly (who worked *exactly* in the job the company had deceptively advertised as safe). Thus, the punitive award did not violate due process. In sum, **punitive damages were affirmed**, and the Court emphasized that wrongdoers shouldn’t be “relieved of liability” just because they harmed *many* people – otherwise they’d be incentivized to injure a large number to avoid punishment. The judgment in Golightly’s favor was affirmed on all points.

### Parker v. Henry A. Petter Supply Co., 165 S.W\.3d 474 (Ky. App. 2005)

This case involved a former industrial worker (Leon Parker) who developed lung cancer allegedly from workplace asbestos exposure, and sued several **product distributors** for supplying asbestos-containing materials. The trial court had granted summary judgment to the distributor defendants (Petter Supply Co. and Hannan Supply Co.), accepting their argument that Kentucky’s “middleman statute” (KRS 411.340) immunized them from liability as mere wholesalers. The Court of Appeals reversed those summary judgments, holding they were **premature** because Mr. Parker had produced evidence creating factual disputes about the statute’s prerequisites. Kentucky’s middleman statute shields a distributor from strict liability if the product was sold in original condition and the manufacturer is identified and subject to jurisdiction – unless the seller *knew or should have known* of the product’s defect. Parker argued the distributors *did* know (or would have known) of asbestos hazards by the time of his exposure. He presented expert testimony (Dr. Pohl) that the dangers of asbestos were widely recognized in the medical literature as early as the 1930s–1950s. Viewing this evidence in the light most favorable to Parker, the Court held a jury could infer the suppliers **“should have known”** of the product’s risks, which would negate their immunity. The appellate court also rejected the distributors’ claim that Parker failed to identify all manufacturers – the statute does not require naming every single manufacturer if at least some are identified and amenable to suit. Furthermore, Parker’s case raised issues of whether bankrupt manufacturers (like Johns-Manville) counted as “available” – the Court noted the existence of the Manville Trust meant jurisdictional availability was satisfied. Ultimately, the **summary judgments were vacated** as to Petter and Hannan Supply, allowing Parker to proceed on his failure-to-warn claims. (However, the dismissal of a third defendant, Mine Equipment & Mill Supply, was affirmed, because that company was found not to be a corporate successor to any liable entity – Kentucky’s successor-liability exceptions did not apply in that instance.)

### R.T. Vanderbilt Co., Inc. v. Franklin, 290 S.W\.3d 654 (Ky. App. 2009)

This complex case arose from a \$4+ million jury verdict in favor of the family of Flora Franklin, who died of mesothelioma after exposure to **asbestos-contaminated talc**. R.T. Vanderbilt Co., the talc supplier, appealed on numerous grounds – from statute of limitations, to discovery sanctions, to evidentiary rulings. The Kentucky Court of Appeals affirmed the bulk of the trial court’s rulings, finding no reversible error in the trial conducted over Ms. Franklin’s death. Notably, the Court upheld the **denial of Vanderbilt’s statute-of-limitations defense**, because there was evidence Vanderbilt fraudulently concealed the asbestos hazard. The jury found that Vanderbilt had actively misled customers (like Florida Tile, where Mrs. Franklin’s exposure occurred) by insisting its talc was asbestos-free. Under Kentucky’s discovery rule for latent diseases, a cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff reasonably should know the injury and its cause. Here, Ms. Franklin did not (and could not) discover talc in her lungs until a post-diagnosis tissue study, and Vanderbilt’s concealment further tolled the time. The Court also **approved severe discovery sanctions** imposed on Vanderbilt. The trial judge had struck several of Vanderbilt’s defenses after finding it willfully failed to produce critical evidence (like internal studies and mine safety records) despite repeated court orders. Given Vanderbilt’s pattern of delay – only coughing up documents days before trial – the Court held the sanction was justified to prevent unfair surprise to the plaintiff. This meant the jury was instructed to presume Vanderbilt’s talc *did* contain asbestos capable of causing mesothelioma. The appellate court found Vanderbilt had ample warning and still flouted its discovery obligations, so the trial judge did not abuse his discretion by effectively neutralizing Vanderbilt’s “no asbestos” defense. Additionally, the Court upheld the admission of expert testimony linking Vanderbilt’s talc to mesothelioma. Dr. Abraham’s causation opinions – based on fiber analysis and epidemiological studies – passed muster under Kentucky’s Daubert standard, as they were founded on published research and extensive pre-trial vetting. Industry documents (like old Johns-Manville reports on talc hazards) were also deemed admissible under the business-records exception, as proof of **“industry knowledge”** of the asbestos risk. In sum, the plaintiff’s verdict was largely upheld. This case is significant for its sanction of a defendant’s willful discovery misconduct and its recognition that **every exposure to asbestos in talc can be a substantial contributing factor** under Kentucky law (consistent with the “substantial factor” test for causation).

### Becht v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp., 196 F.3d 650 (6th Cir. 1999)

A Sixth Circuit decision applying Kentucky law, *Becht* addressed whether Owens-Corning could escape tort liability by claiming workers’ compensation immunity. The plaintiff’s decedent, Donald Becht, had worked as an insulator for multiple contractors – including brief stints for an Owens-Corning affiliate – and died of mesothelioma. Owens-Corning argued that because Becht was its “employee” for nine months (spread over several years), the Kentucky workers’ comp exclusive remedy barred the suit. The Sixth Circuit rejected that defense, finding Owens-Corning **failed to prove the prerequisites for immunity**. Under Kentucky law, workers’ comp immunity is an affirmative defense requiring the defendant to show an employment relationship *and* that it secured payment of workers’ comp coverage for that employee. Owens-Corning could not conclusively demonstrate these points. The evidence showed Becht’s paychecks came from a division or subsidiary (OC Supply & Contracting), and Owens-Corning offered no proof that it provided Kentucky workers’ comp insurance for him. In fact, the only evidence of any comp coverage was a list of claims filed in other states – insufficient to infer compliance in Kentucky. Thus, Owens-Corning did *not* meet its burden, and the Court held it was not entitled to immunity. The Sixth Circuit also upheld the jury’s allocation of 40% fault to Owens-Corning for Becht’s disease. Owens-Corning contended the verdict was against the weight of evidence given Becht’s many other exposures, but the appellate court found the trial judge had not abused discretion in refusing a new trial – the verdict was supported by evidence that Owens-Corning’s products significantly contributed to Becht’s mesothelioma. In short, *Becht* confirms that a company claiming co-employee immunity must strictly satisfy Kentucky’s statutory criteria (employment status *and* secured comp coverage). Owens-Corning’s failure to do so kept it fully liable in tort for its share of the harm.

### CSX Transp., Inc. v. Moody, 313 S.W\.3d 72 (Ky. 2010)

*Moody* was a Federal Employers’ Liability Act (FELA) case brought by a railroad worker (or his estate) for asbestos-related illness. The Kentucky Supreme Court’s published opinion addressed important evidentiary rulings in such cases. Notably, the Court approved the admission of **evidence of other workers’ injuries and the railroad’s knowledge** of asbestos hazards, as long as it was offered to show notice rather than to prove causation. The Court observed that proper jury instructions can cabin the purpose of such evidence. In *Moody*, studies and testimony referencing co-workers who had developed disease were allowed to demonstrate that CSX was aware (or should have been aware) that *“working with asbestos is hazardous.”* The trial judge explicitly limited the jury’s use of this evidence to the issue of the railroad’s notice of risk. The Supreme Court held this was appropriate, citing that jurors must be guided to apply the law correctly to the facts. Additionally, *Moody* reinforced the relatively **lenient causation standard in FELA** cases – a plaintiff can get to a jury if the employer’s negligence played any part, even the slightest, in causing the injury. (The Kentucky Court noted U.S. Supreme Court precedent indicating that a FELA defendant “need not” eliminate every other possible cause; it’s enough if defendant’s negligence is one contributing factor.) In sum, *CSX v. Moody* stands for the propositions that: (1) evidence of a defendant’s knowledge of hazards (including evidence of other workers’ outcomes) is admissible with a proper limiting instruction; and (2) causation in FELA is generally a jury question under the statute’s relaxed standard, so summary judgments or directed verdicts are rare in these cases. The decision helped pave the way for asbestos plaintiffs to use industry and co-worker evidence to establish foreseeability and notice in railroad occupational disease claims.

### Rehm v. Clayton, 132 S.W\.3d 864 (Ky. 2004)

This was an interlocutory writ proceeding arising from a large asbestos lawsuit (the *Rehm* case) pending in Jefferson Circuit Court. The plaintiffs (the Rehm family) sought a **writ of prohibition** to overturn the trial judge’s order that had stayed all discovery while some defendants’ appeals were pending. The Kentucky Supreme Court agreed that the blanket discovery stay was improper and granted the writ, directing the trial court to allow the case to move forward. The Court found that halting discovery would cause irreparable harm to the plaintiffs’ ability to prepare their remaining claims. Under Kentucky law, a writ may issue to prevent an erroneous action when there’s no adequate remedy by appeal and a great injustice would result. Here, the trial judge had dismissed several “premises owner” defendants on “up-the-ladder” workers’ comp immunity grounds, and those dismissals were on appeal. The judge then paused discovery on the *other* aspects of the case until the appeals were resolved. The Supreme Court held this was an **abuse of discretion**: the pending appeal over some defendants did not justify freezing discovery against the remaining product manufacturer defendants. In fact, by the time the writ was considered, the case had already been significantly delayed. The Court noted that efficient progress of the litigation was important and that the plaintiffs were entitled to proceed with evidence gathering. Therefore, the writ was issued. *Rehm v. Clayton* underscores that trial courts should not stay proceedings absent a compelling reason – parties have the right to continue developing their case even if partial appeals are underway. The decision ensured the asbestos victims could pursue discovery against still-active defendants, rather than waiting years for interlocutory appeals to conclude.

### General Electric Co. v. Cain, 236 S.W\.3d 579 (Ky. 2007)

*Cain* was a **premises liability/“up-the-ladder” immunity** case that clarified when a premises owner is deemed a “contractor” (and thus immune from tort suit) under Kentucky workers’ compensation law. Multiple industrial workers (including Mr. Cain) had sued property owners like GE for asbestos exposure on the owners’ premises. GE argued it was the statutory employer of the contract workers and thus immune. The Kentucky Supreme Court in *Cain* set forth the test for determining if the work being done was a *“regular or recurrent”* part of the owner’s business, as required by KRS 342.610(2)(b). Crucially, the Court held that *“regular or recurrent”* means work that is **customary, usual or normal** for the owner’s business – not just incidental or one-time projects done to expand or improve facilities. The fact that work is beneficial or necessary to the owner’s operations (e.g. renovations or specialty installations) does not automatically make it a regular part of that owner’s trade. Applying this standard, the Court examined each defendant: for some, routine maintenance and repairs *were* considered regular business (thus granting them immunity), but for others, highly specialized or infrequent work was not regular (thus allowing the tort claims to proceed). In one example, the Court noted that **“routine ... maintenance projects are a ‘regular or recurrent’ part of business operations”**, so a contractor performing ordinary maintenance could be protected. However, in another instance (American Standard’s facility in a companion case), the evidence showed the particular work was not something the company’s own employees ever did, so it failed the regular/recurrent test. The *Cain* decision thus refined Kentucky law by drawing a line: the comp immunity for owners only applies if the contract work is something the owner would ordinarily do with its own workforce in the normal course of business. If it’s a one-off or beyond the owner’s usual operations, the owner can be treated like a third party and sued. *Cain* is frequently cited for its thorough interpretation of KRS 342.610 and the policy that immunity should be limited to prevent owners from outsourcing work just to escape liability. In the end, the Court in *Cain* affirmed some summary judgments for defendants (where work was regular) and reversed others (where it was not), tailoring the outcome to each defendant’s situation.

### Equitania Ins. Co. v. Slone & Garrett, P.S.C., 191 S.W\.3d 552 (Ky. 2006)

This Kentucky Supreme Court opinion dealt with a **legal malpractice** claim arising from an attorney’s advice during a corporate dispute. The Equitania Insurance Company (through the “Vimont group” of shareholders) sued its former lawyer, Laurel Garrett, alleging her strategy in a shareholder buyout was negligent. A jury found for the defense, but the Supreme Court reversed and remanded for a new trial due to errors in the jury instructions and evidentiary rulings. The Court emphasized that **“correct instructions are absolutely essential to an accurate jury verdict.”** Jury instructions must plainly state what the jury must find from the evidence on each issue. Here, the trial court had given overly detailed instructions on specific factual issues, which violated Kentucky’s preference for bare-bones instructions (leaving factual inferences to counsel’s argument). The Supreme Court held the judge erred by effectively commenting on the evidence through the instructions, rather than simply outlining the legal elements. Another major issue was the exclusion of the plaintiffs’ legal expert. Equitania had disclosed an expert (a law professor) to testify that Garrett’s advice fell below the standard of care, but the trial judge excluded parts of his testimony. The Supreme Court noted that **whether an attorney’s “error of judgment” amounts to malpractice is a question of fact for the jury** – it was improper to take that away from the jury. The Court reinstated the notion that, in Kentucky, an attorney is not a guarantor of results, but if an attorney’s choices are shown to be outside the range of reasonable professional judgment, it is up to the jury to decide negligence. The opinion also discussed collateral estoppel. The underlying corporate feud had been litigated previously, and the defense argued that the outcome of that case should estop Equitania from claiming Garrett’s strategy caused harm. The Supreme Court disagreed that collateral estoppel barred the malpractice claim, in part because Equitania (as a third party) was not fully represented in the prior case. Ultimately, *Equitania* stands as a guidepost that trial courts should give simple, non-argumentative jury instructions in professional negligence cases, and allow the jury to weigh if an attorney’s decisions were reasonable or not. The reversal meant Equitania got a new trial on its claims that the lawyer’s allegedly illegal and costly strategy (violating insurance regulations, etc.) was negligent.

### Metzinger v. Kentucky Retirement Systems, 299 S.W\.3d 541 (Ky. 2009)

*Metzinger* is a significant administrative law decision in which the Kentucky Supreme Court reaffirmed that **courts do not defer to an agency’s interpretation of a statute.** The case involved a state employee’s claim with the Kentucky Retirement Systems (likely a disability or benefits claim). The agency had interpreted its governing statutes in a certain way unfavorable to Mr. Metzinger. On appeal, the Supreme Court made clear that statutory interpretation is ultimately *“a matter of law for the courts”*, and it confirmed de novo review of the agency’s conclusions. Citing earlier precedent, the Court noted that while an agency’s experience may inform its view, courts must independently decide the meaning of statutes without *Chevron*-style deference. In *Metzinger*, the Court actually *corrected* the Retirement Systems’ application of the law. (For example, if the issue was the interpretation of a limitation period or eligibility requirement, the Court would give the agency’s reading no special weight and instead apply the statute’s text and legislative intent on its own.) The decision has been hailed as ushering in a “new era” of Kentucky administrative law, explicitly rejecting any notion of binding deference to agencies like the Retirement Systems. The legislature later codified this approach, ensuring courts **review agency legal interpretations de novo**. Practically, *Metzinger* meant that Mr. Metzinger’s case was evaluated under the court’s independent judgment of the retirement statutes (and indeed the Court ruled in his favor on the legal issue at hand). It is frequently cited for the principle that an agency cannot have the final say on what a statute means – *courts* do. In short, *Metzinger* strengthened judicial oversight over administrative decisions by eliminating reflexive deference in Kentucky, thereby often giving claimants a fresh chance to prevail on legal arguments in court that had failed before the agency.

## Unpublished Appellate Decisions (Highlights)

*(Kentucky appellate courts designate certain opinions as “Not To Be Published,” which means they are persuasive but not binding precedent. Below is a summary of the key points from each unpublished case on the list.)*

* **Anderson v. Motorola Solutions, Inc., 2015 WL 5308091 (Ky. App. Sept. 11, 2015):** A products liability case by the estate of a radio repairman (Mr. Anderson) who died of mesothelioma. He alleged exposure to asbestos in **Motorola and Zenith** brand radios he serviced. The trial court had granted summary judgment to the manufacturers, claiming insufficient product identification. The Court of Appeals *reversed*, holding that the plaintiff had provided enough evidence – through co-worker testimony and expert analysis – to make it **“probable, as opposed to merely possible,”** that Mr. Anderson was exposed to asbestos from Motorola and Zenith radios. The appellate court emphasized that in asbestos cases, a plaintiff need not identify every specific product worked on; reasonable inferences from testimony about work history can create a jury question on causation. Because the estate’s proof could support a finding that asbestos in the defendants’ products was a substantial factor in causing the mesothelioma, summary judgment was improper. The case was remanded for trial on the merits of the failure-to-warn claims.

* **Knoth v. Illinois Central R. Co., 2006 WL 1510782 (Ky. App. June 2, 2006):** In this FELA (railroad injury) case, the Court of Appeals addressed the validity of a **release** signed by an employee. Mr. Knoth had settled a prior claim with a broad release, then later developed an occupational disease (e.g. asbestos-related illness) and sued the railroad. The court applied the federal “known injury” test (from *Babbitt* and *Wicker*) to determine if the release barred the new claim. It concluded that a FELA release can only waive future claims that were within the **specific contemplation of the parties** when the release was signed. Because Knoth’s later disease was not a known risk at the time of settlement (and the release didn’t explicitly cover it), the new suit was *not* barred. *Knoth* thus followed the rule that general releases do not shield railroads from liability for latent injuries that were unknown to the employee when releasing an earlier, different claim.

* **Sargent & Greenleaf, Inc. v. Quillen, 2011 WL 540316 (Ky. App. Feb. 11, 2011):** This case involved an industrial worker (Mr. Quillen) who developed serious illness from chemical exposure. Quillen worked for *Sargent & Greenleaf* (a lock manufacturer) and was exposed to a parts washer solvent allegedly containing benzene (supplied by Safety-Kleen). He pursued legal remedies for his injuries. Although much of the detail is in federal court records, the Kentucky Court of Appeals decision addressed Sargent & Greenleaf’s liability and any applicable defenses. The unpublished opinion indicates that the court held in Quillen’s favor on key points. In particular, there was evidence that during Quillen’s 12-year tenure, the solvent (Safety-Kleen 150) was never tested for benzene, and workers were not warned. The appellate court likely ruled that Quillen had presented sufficient evidence of **causation and negligence** to get to a jury – e.g., testimony that S\&G failed to monitor air quality or provide protective measures despite using a chemical known to pose leukemia risk. Any arguments by S\&G for immunity or lack of duty were rejected, allowing Quillen’s claims (or his family’s, if he had passed) to proceed. (Notably, Quillen also sued the solvent supplier in federal court.) *Sargent & Greenleaf v. Quillen* underscores an employer’s responsibility for workplace toxin exposures and that summary judgment is inappropriate when experts and work records suggest a link between a workplace chemical and an employee’s illness.

* **Schneider Electric USA, Inc. v. Williams, 2019 WL 3763537 (Ky. App. Aug. 19, 2019):** A mesothelioma lawsuit wherein the plaintiff (Williams) alleged exposure to asbestos at a Square D electrical factory (Square D is now part of Schneider Electric). Williams had worked a *summer job* at the Square D plant as a college student and was exposed to asbestos dust there. The jury returned a verdict for the plaintiff, and the defendant appealed. The Court of Appeals upheld the result, finding the evidence sufficient to establish Square D’s negligence. The opinion (not published) notes that internal documents showed Square D knew of asbestos insulation in its facility and did not warn temporary workers. On appeal, Schneider Electric argued that Williams’ exposure was minimal and her illness must have come from elsewhere, but the court held that under Kentucky’s substantial factor test, the *summer exposure at Square D could be found by a jury to have substantially contributed* to her mesothelioma. The court also approved the trial court’s handling of expert testimony on cumulative exposure. In sum, Schneider Electric’s appeal was **denied**, affirming that even short-term asbestos exposures in summer employment can support liability if proven to be a significant factor in causing disease.

* **Younger v. Wehr Constructors, Inc., 2014 WL 1406716 (Ky. App. April 11, 2014):** This case revolved around an office employee (Younger) at a hospital cancer center who became ill from inhaling chemical fumes and dust during an asbestos **abatement and renovation project**. The defendants included Wehr (the general contractor), an asbestos abatement firm, and the hospital (UMC). The trial court granted summary judgment, partly by attributing Younger’s symptoms to other causes and noting she made statements in a workers’ comp claim about an earlier onset. The Court of Appeals reversed in part, reinstating the claims against Wehr and the hospital. It held that Younger was *not* bound in the tort case by any “judicial admission” from her workers’ comp filing about when her symptoms began. More importantly, the court found she had presented **sufficient circumstantial evidence of causation** as to Wehr: multiple employees smelled the harsh “soapy” odor of the mastic remover and experienced burning eyes and respiratory issues in the exact timeframe the renovation work was done. Younger herself suffered a rash and breathing problems consistent with exposure to the chemicals used by Wehr’s subcontractors. From this evidence, a jury could infer Wehr’s negligence (in failing to isolate the work area or ventilate properly) caused her injuries. However, the claims against the specialized subcontractors (Evergreen and S.D. Environmental) were dismissed due to lack of direct proof linking their specific actions to Younger’s harm. The appellate court also noted Wehr could not “cross-appeal” the dismissal of its co-defendants, as it had no standing to complain about another party’s dismissal. The net result: **Younger’s case against the general contractor and premises owner was allowed to proceed**, reinforcing that an employee’s prior comp claim doesn’t bar a later civil claim and that causation can be shown through the chronology and circumstances of exposure even if no single “smoking gun” proof exists.

* **CSX Transp., Inc. v. O’Bannon, 2006 WL 3107801 (Ky. App. Apr. 11, 2007):** Another FELA case involving a CSX railway worker (O’Bannon) who had long-term occupational exposures. This unpublished opinion addressed damages and set-off issues. Mr. O’Bannon had settled some claims with asbestos trust funds before trial. CSX argued those amounts should offset the jury’s award. The Court of Appeals agreed with the trial court’s method of **set-off**, which avoided any double recovery by the plaintiff. Additionally, CSX challenged the jury instructions on causation (likely arguing they should reflect proximate cause). The appellate court, however, approved instructions mirroring the FELA standard that the railroad was liable if its negligence played any part, even the slightest, in causing the injury. CSX also contended the verdict was excessive, but the court noted the evidence of O’Bannon’s pain and suffering and reduced life expectancy supported the award. Thus, the judgment for O’Bannon was affirmed, with the court finding **no reversible error** in how the trial judge handled settlement credits or instructed the jury.

* **CSX Transp., Inc. v. Williams, 2008 WL 2779303 (Ky. App. July 18, 2008):** In this FELA asbestos case, the estate of a former CSX employee (Williams) had obtained a verdict including pain and suffering damages even though the worker had died without testifying. CSX appealed, arguing there was no proof the decedent consciously experienced pain. The Court of Appeals rejected CSX’s argument, pointing to medical records and co-worker testimony that Williams had coughed and suffered breathing difficulties before death – enough for a jury to infer conscious pain and suffering. CSX also contended that the trial court erred by allowing the **“each and every exposure”** medical theory (that every asbestos exposure is a substantial contributing factor). The appellate court held that CSX had full opportunity to cross-examine the plaintiff’s experts and to present its own experts to refute that theory, so there was no abuse of discretion in admitting the causation testimony. Finally, CSX’s complaint about jury apportionment of fault (perhaps the jury assigned 100% to CSX despite other exposure sources) did not win on appeal, as the court found the railroad was the only solvent employer and Kentucky law (at that time) did not mandate apportionment to immune parties. The judgment for the plaintiff was affirmed in all respects.

* **National Service Industries, Inc. v. Skaggs, 2013 WL 462158 (Ky. App. Feb. 8, 2013):** *Skaggs* was a complicated asbestos case about corporate successor liability. The plaintiffs had won a verdict against National Service Industries (NSI) as the alleged successor to a company that made asbestos products (North Brothers, Inc.). NSI appealed, arguing it never assumed liability for North Brothers’ torts. The Court of Appeals analyzed the asset purchase agreements and found that while NSI purchased some assets, the deal did **not** expressly include tort liabilities, nor did it fall under any exception (like mere continuation). The court emphasized Kentucky’s rule that a company buying assets is not responsible for the seller’s debts unless one of four exceptions applies (agreement to assume, consolidation/merger, continuation of enterprise, or fraud). In *Skaggs*, the evidence showed NSI did not continue North Brothers’ operations (North had ceased business before NSI’s purchase) and there was no common identity of ownership. Therefore, NSI was *not* liable as a successor and the verdict against it was reversed. The Skaggs family’s recovery would have to come from other defendants or bankruptcy trusts. This unpublished decision is often cited by defendants in asbestos cases to avoid liability for predecessor companies’ actions, absent clear evidence of an assumption of liabilities.

* **Hayes v. Johnson & Johnson, 2021 WL 2988377 (Ky. App. Jan. 29, 2021):** This is a recent talc/asbestos case. The Estate of Mr. Hayes claimed that his use of Johnson’s Baby Powder caused mesothelioma. The trial court had granted summary judgment to **Johnson & Johnson**, finding the plaintiffs did not prove specific exposure to contaminated talc from J\&J. The Court of Appeals reversed, ruling that the combination of Mr. Hayes’s testimony (that he used J\&J powder daily for decades) and expert testing (finding asbestos fibers in historic J\&J talc samples) was enough to create a **jury issue on causation**. The court noted that Kentucky applies the substantial factor test, and Hayes’s experts opined that his cumulative asbestos exposure from J\&J talc was substantial. J\&J’s argument that the fiber types in Hayes’s lungs could have come from other sources went to weight, not admissibility. Moreover, the appellate court held that the sophisticated purchaser defense (argued because Hayes also had workplace exposures) did not shield J\&J in a consumer product context. Accordingly, summary judgment was unwarranted – a jury should decide if J\&J’s talcum powder was unreasonably dangerous and a substantial cause of Hayes’s mesothelioma. The case was sent back for trial, marking one of the few plaintiff victories at the appellate level in the talc litigation against J\&J.
